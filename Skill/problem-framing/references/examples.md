# Problem Framing Examples

Complete worked examples showing good vs. bad problem framing.

---

## Example 1: Task Management App

### ❌ Bad Problem Framing

**Problem Statement:**
"People need better task management"

**Why it's bad:**
- Not specific about WHO
- Not specific about WHAT problem
- Not specific about WHEN it occurs
- No evidence or validation

---

### ✅ Good Problem Framing

**Problem Statement:**
Freelance designers lose track of client deliverables when juggling 3+ projects simultaneously, leading to missed deadlines and damaged client relationships.

**Target User:**
Freelance designers (graphic, web, UX) working independently with 3-10 active clients

**Characteristics:**
- Work from home or co-working spaces
- Use email, Slack, and Notion but lack unified view
- Prioritize creative work over administrative tasks
- Fear missing deadlines more than being disorganized

**Jobs to Be Done:**
- **Functional:** Track all client deliverables across multiple projects without switching contexts
- **Emotional:** Feel confident I haven't forgotten anything important
- **Social:** Appear professional and organized to clients

**Current Alternatives:**
- Spreadsheets (forgotten when not open)
- Email starring (buried in inbox)
- Notion databases (too much overhead to maintain)
- Mental tracking (doesn't scale past 3 projects)

**Trigger Moments:**
- Monday mornings planning the week
- When a client asks for a status update
- After onboarding a new client
- When feeling overwhelmed by competing deadlines

**Key Assumptions:**
- Designers will check the tool daily
- Clients provide clear deliverable lists upfront
- Designers have authority to negotiate deadlines
- The pain is severe enough to adopt new software

**Success Criteria:**
- Zero missed deadlines reported in user testing
- Users check tool at least 5x per week
- Time spent on admin tasks decreases by 30%

**Open Questions:**
- How do designers currently negotiate scope creep?
- What happens when a client changes requirements mid-project?
- Would they pay $10/month for this solution?

## Example 2: Developer Documentation Tool

### ❌ Bad Problem Framing

**Problem Statement:**
"Documentation is hard"

**Why it's bad:**
- Too vague (what kind of documentation?)
- No specific user
- No context about when this is a problem
- Could apply to any domain

---

### ✅ Good Problem Framing

**Problem Statement:**
Junior developers at fast-growing startups spend 40% of their onboarding time hunting for architecture decisions that aren't documented anywhere, slowing their time-to-first-commit from weeks to months.

**Target User:**
Junior developers (0-2 years experience) joining engineering teams of 10-50 people

**Characteristics:**
- First or second professional dev job
- Expected to contribute within 30 days
- Afraid to ask "dumb questions" repeatedly
- Strong at coding, weak at navigating organizational knowledge
- Prefer written docs over scheduled meetings

**Jobs to Be Done:**
- **Functional:** Understand why the codebase is architected a specific way without asking a senior dev
- **Emotional:** Feel confident in my understanding before making changes
- **Social:** Appear competent and self-sufficient to my team

**Current Alternatives:**
- Asking teammates in Slack (feels like bothering them)
- Digging through old PRs (time-consuming, unclear if still relevant)
- Reading code comments (sparse and outdated)
- Tribal knowledge from onboarding buddy (inconsistent, dependent on who you get)

**Trigger Moments:**
- First week on the job
- Before making first architectural decision
- When encountering unexpected patterns in code
- After a deploy fails due to misunderstanding

**Key Assumptions:**
- Senior devs will document decisions if it's low-friction
- Juniors actually want to find answers themselves vs. asking
- Architecture decisions are stable enough to document
- Teams have 30 minutes/week for documentation

**Success Criteria:**
- Junior dev time-to-first-commit reduced from 4 weeks to 2 weeks
- Slack questions about architecture decisions drop by 50%
- 90% of major decisions documented within 24 hours

**Open Questions:**
- Who maintains documentation when team members leave?
- How do we handle evolving/deprecated decisions?
- Would engineering managers pay for this?

## Example 3: Recipe Scaling App

### ❌ Bad Problem Framing

**Problem Statement:**
"Cooking recipes are confusing"

**Why it's bad:**
- Not specific about the confusion
- No clear user segment
- Missing the actual problem context

---

### ✅ Good Problem Framing

**Problem Statement:**
Home cooks preparing meals for families of 5+ waste ingredients and time when online recipes serve 2-4 people, requiring mental math that leads to measurement errors and failed dishes.

**Target User:**
Parents (age 30-45) cooking dinner for families of 5-7 people, 5+ nights per week

**Characteristics:**
- Limited time (30-45 min for dinner prep)
- Budget-conscious (meal planning to minimize waste)
- Moderate cooking skills
- Use Pinterest/AllRecipes for recipe discovery
- Cook while managing kids/homework/chaos

**Jobs to Be Done:**
- **Functional:** Scale recipe ingredient quantities accurately without manual calculation
- **Emotional:** Feel confident the dish will turn out correctly
- **Social:** Feed my family healthy, tasty meals consistently

**Current Alternatives:**
- Mental math while cooking (error-prone, stressful)
- Doubling recipe and doing fractions (1.5x eggs = ??)
- Buying more ingredients than needed (wasteful)
- Avoiding recipes that don't scale well

**Trigger Moments:**
- Sunday meal planning for the week
- Finding a recipe that looks perfect but serves 4
- Mid-cooking when realizing quantities are wrong
- After a failed dish due to scaling errors

**Key Assumptions:**
- Families consistently cook the same serving sizes
- Users find recipes online (not cookbooks)
- Measurement errors are a real pain point
- Users will input data while cooking (hands busy)

**Success Criteria:**
- Recipe scaling errors drop to zero
- Users save 5+ minutes per recipe
- 80% reduction in ingredient waste
- Users cook new recipes 2x more frequently

**Open Questions:**
- How do users discover new recipes?
- Would they pay or is this ad-supported?
- Do they scale up or down more often?
- What about dietary restrictions/substitutions?

## Pattern Recognition

### Elements of Strong Problem Framing:

1. **Specific WHO**: Job title, demographic, behavior, not "everyone"
2. **Specific WHAT**: The actual problem, with measurable impact
3. **Specific WHEN**: The moment/context when the problem occurs
4. **Evidence**: Numbers, quotes, observable behaviors
5. **Alternatives**: What they do today (reveals market dynamics)
6. **JTBD**: Functional, emotional, and social dimensions
7. **Assumptions**: What must be true, prioritized by risk
8. **Success criteria**: Measurable outcomes

### Red Flags in Bad Problem Framing:

- ❌ "Users want..." (not a problem)
- ❌ "People need..." (too vague)
- ❌ "Everyone struggles with..." (not specific)
- ❌ No mention of current alternatives
- ❌ No evidence cited
- ❌ Solution embedded in problem statement
- ❌ Multiple unrelated problems bundled together
